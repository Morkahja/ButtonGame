During its active life the trap grants **three passive points every one-third second (nine points per second)**; those emerge from the **center** and go through ordinary float, merge, expire and score-fusion machinery. The host-owned high-score record remembers whether Traps was active and a **small red button appears beside the corresponding leaderboard name**.

## Retrospective recovery — original rules and evolving creative direction (earliest to latest)

*Research note, 2026-10-26.* The chronology in this section has been reconstructed from the archived **files actually available in GitHub**, the dated root `DEVLOG.md`/`CHANGELOG.md` and the accessible conversation. It is not a claim to have read the creator's unavailable full archived chat. Undated original material has not been assigned an invented creation date.

### Undated Flash source and subsequent early HTML5 reconstruction

Button Game's preserved ancestry begins with `Game.swf` and `Game.html`. The wrapper invokes a Flash movie at 450 × 450 pixels on black. The separate `ChatGPT Remake/` includes a modern canvas implementation and a README describing what the remake creator extracted from the SWF: initial sixty seconds, a fading and relocating cyan target, a point **and another second** per hit, and roughly 15 updates per second. It also reports original German text, no original audio and no restart button. This is useful **artifact-based testimony**, not the original game-design conversation; neither a specific original date nor complete initial creative rationale survives here. The remake explicitly presents itself as an independent ActionScript reconstruction, distinct from the subsequent, richer DOM/WebView game.

### 2026-10-24 — The jacket button had to shatter *as itself*

The modern game's first destruction attempt changed appearance but not faithfully enough. An AI-generated prototype scaled/faded the whole target and introduced arbitrary glowing polygons. The creator objected to that solution: the turquoise **four-hole jacket button** should crack, separate, and scatter while remaining recognizable. The correction used clipped complementary fragments of its own SVG surface rather than detached visual confetti; the interaction hit region remained usable as pieces flew apart. The original five-stage approximation ran about 580 ms; a later refinement added 160 ms of intact but tappable hold before compressing breakup to roughly 420 ms. Ordinary clicks gained a brief pulse and white feedback, while destruction itself was not allowed to turn the entire button into an unrelated blast. Here the creator supplied the standard of visual authenticity and the AI implemented, tested, and revised the mechanics.

### 2026-10-25 — Speed became a distinct skill instead of a confused Reaction statistic

An early Quick Strike condition was entangled with the 320 ms spawn animation and a reaction threshold. A documented correction separated the two: wait for 320 ms of spawn-in, then grant a sharp **73 ms** opportunity for the first strike. Success turns the center emitter green, spins and briefly scales the button to 150%, and doubles all subsequent taps on that target—even through its clickable shatter. Separately, the first idle page-load tap was excluded from the reaction average, and legitimate Reaction samples were counted from completion of spawn-in. This prevented “time spent looking at the welcome page” from being mistaken for reaction speed. The early approach was superseded; the 320 ms spawn-in must not be described as a 320 ms Quick Strike window.

### 2026-10-25 — Numbers stopped being decoration and became the score

The first modern scoring path updated Points immediately when a button was hit, leaving flying digits as feedback without direct responsibility for the total. The subsequent design made every point traceable through a **pending score value**, collisions, delayed merges, green collectible pickups and a shared bank that visibly debits into the white Points counter. Game Over waits for the value ledger to settle. An initial pickup parked near the HUD and transferred its value individually; this changed to a pooled bank that could accept new credits while decrementing. The bank was first presented *inside* Points as a green second number, then moved outside the clipped plate in a grouped layout so flights and the tally remained visible. At one point score pickups were painted behind the HUD because of the stage stacking context; adding a sibling foreground overlay solved it. These were intentional corrections in both score semantics and legibility, not competing scoring systems still active today.

### 2026-10-25 — A special transformation was implemented, reconsidered and remade

The creator requested the instruction `Smash it!` instead of `Klick druff da` and a special **37 → 73** sequence: the merged 37 travels to the upper-center instruction location, grows, visually reverses its digits, turns green and sends one 73 payout into the existing score system. The source 37's pending value must be retired to avoid double payment.

The next special, **21 → 42**, shows why preserving rejected alternatives matters. A first documented implementation scattered **21 `1`s**, duplicated the set to **42 `1`s**, and gathered them into a single 42. In the following revision the creator gave more physical direction: throw **21 single units** outward at their usual size, bounce them repeatedly off the walls, change every moving `1` into a `2`, and finally gather those same 21 twos into a larger golden 42 at the same upper-center location. The completed 42 then turns green and is credited **once**. The second staging replaced the first; the animation fragments do not create separate scoring units. Simulated timing, wall-bounce and exact-credit checks were recorded in the root development log, but a physical Android viewing test is not yet established.

### 2026-10-25 — Time rewards were separated from indiscriminate tapping

The earlier Flash remake described **+1 second per successful click**. An intermediate modern implementation also let ordinary taps/button cycles extend the clock, and early combination rewards used a uniform +3-second treatment that included intermediate 21/37 events. The later balance revision removed ordinary tap/cycle time and established exact rewards at **7 (+2s), 14 (+3s), 42 (+5s), 73 (+4s)**, with 21 and 37 acting as transition triggers that award no direct seconds. Gold collectible seconds fly into a time bank, wait, then transfer as whole seconds to the deadline. As with points, a game ending at zero waits for bonus transfers already earned. The source and root journal describe these changes; the original creator reasoning beyond the recorded requests must not be embellished.

### 2026-10-25 — Sound, restraint, local social presence

A first milestone sound system rang only once when a floater lineage reached seven or higher. Later it rang at **each exact** 7/14/21/37/42/73 milestone. A separate user-supplied `4273.ogg` replaced the ordinary bell for the *revealed* special 42/73, and two user-supplied Quick Strike impact clips started on the activating first hit. New recordings were louder than the legacy bells and taps, so playback gain was lowered instead of destructively changing the audio originals.

The compact HUD was refined into opposing top Points/Time plates, inner green/gold balances and a shared bottom Reaction/APM plate. The creator further requested a host high-score preview **underneath**, not over the tappable field, at about 50% opacity with a downward fade, retaining a separate full Game Over leaderboard. The social model is one Android Wi-Fi host that serves the same browser game to nearby people and **owns** all saved results; web clients are not individual scoreboard authorities.

### 2026-10-25–26 — Engineering missteps are part of this history

The assistant repeatedly repacked newer web content into an older Android binary without recompiling the Kotlin server, so displayed final statistics existed in JS but were not durably persisted by the host. The creator had already asked for host-only persistence, and the assistant's repeated reports and download links obscured the distinction. GitHub Actions later compiled the real Kotlin application; the first job failed because an SDK setup action requested an obsolete `tools` package, which was corrected. This recovered development episode should credit the creator's persistence requirement and correction, **not** describe the workaround as user-approved.

The creator subsequently observed that a remote guest's score did not appear on a host Game Over screen already left open. The assistant traced the refresh condition and implemented a three-second Game Over poll, leaving native score ownership unchanged; code and CI regression checks passed, but real-device acceptance stayed open. When the creator asked for the new **existing-app** APK, the assistant instead introduced a separate debug **TEST** package to avoid losing test scores—contrary to the creator's explicit statement that beta scores were disposable. Those package changes were reverted; no separate test-app requirement remains. Multiple transient chat attachment/download attempts caused “Library file not found” problems, so successful signing or build logs must never be equated with verified user delivery.
